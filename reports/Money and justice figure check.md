# Both figure pairs hold, but need labels

Neither pair contradicts itself. In each case the two numbers measure different things, and the site needs to say what each one counts. Public Citizen's **$4.2 billion** is money that **515 donors of $1 million or more gave to super PACs** (money raised) through late October 2026. OpenSecrets' **$3.9 billion** is a **projection of what all outside groups will spend** on the 2026 federal elections. A larger amount raised than spent is expected, because super PACs pass money to other committees, cover overhead and keep cash in reserve. The Prison Policy Initiative pair comes from a single report, but it uses two different totals. The **$445 billion** is government spending (**$417.1 billion**) plus about **$27.7 billion paid by people in the system and their families**. The **13%** immigration share is measured against the **government-only $417.1 billion**, not against $445 billion, where it would be about 12.2%. The risk on the site is that a reader treats the two numbers in a pair as parts of one whole. The fix is to label each figure precisely. The PPI figures are confirmed against PPI's own pages. The Public Citizen figures are confirmed against Public Citizen's own report. The OpenSecrets figures are confirmed only through secondary news coverage, because opensecrets.org could not be reached from the research environment.

## Public Citizen's $4.2 billion counts money given, not money spent

Public Citizen's report, "Millionaires and Billionaires Fuel 2026 Super PAC Spending" by Mike Tanglis, is dated October 27, 2026 on the web, and its PDF is headed only "October 2026" ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/millionaires-and-billionaires-fuel-2026-super-pac-spending/)). Despite the word "spending" in the title, every figure in it measures money received. Super PACs **"reported receiving $4.2 billion from 515 donors,"** and those donors account for **"more than 70 percent of the money raised by super PACs"** ([Public Citizen PDF](https://www.citizen.org/wp-content/uploads/1-Million-Super-PAC-Donors-1.pdf)). The infographic gives the share more precisely as **73% of super PAC contributions**. Without Elon Musk, the figure is $3.8 billion from 514 donors ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/millionaires-and-billionaires-fuel-2026-super-pac-spending/)).

The method matters for any comparison. The analysis uses FEC filings for the 2026 cycle "thus far." It **includes hybrid PACs**, combines one donor's gifts across several super PACs, and **excludes transfers from other super PACs** to avoid counting the same money twice ([Public Citizen PDF](https://www.citizen.org/wp-content/uploads/1-Million-Super-PAC-Donors-1.pdf)). The report gives no exact data cutoff date, and the latest source it cites is dated October 23, 2026. It also never states the total that the 73% is a share of. Dividing $4.2 billion by 73% gives roughly **$5.75 to $5.8 billion** in total super PAC and hybrid PAC receipts. That is arithmetic from the research notes, not a published figure, and the site should not present it as Public Citizen's number.

## OpenSecrets' $3.9 billion is projected spending across all outside groups

The $3.9 billion figure reached this research only secondhand. opensecrets.org, its web.archive.org copies and its archive.ph copies all returned a site-blocked error, so no OpenSecrets page was read directly. IBTimes, on November 4, 2026, attributes the following to "an OpenSecrets analysis of Federal Election Commission data." Outside groups' **spending "is expected to reach $3.9 billion, up 85% from four years earlier,"** and **total federal election spending is projected at $11.9 billion** "once all campaign finance reports are filed." IBTimes also notes that some totals may change as more disclosures come in ([IBTimes](https://www.ibtimes.com/2026-midterm-election-spending-record-11-9-billion-3809414)). The BBC, as republished by UA.News, and Al Jazeera both report the roughly $12 billion total from OpenSecrets, but neither carries the $3.9 billion figure ([UA.News/BBC](https://ua.news/en/world/opensecrets-prognozuie-11-9-mlrd-vitrat-na-vibori-u-ssha-bbc-news); [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/3/how-us-midterms-have-cost-a-record-12bn-as-cost-of-living-soars)).

OpenSecrets' 2026 definition of "outside spending" could not be checked. Historically, the organisation has counted **independent expenditures, electioneering communications and communication costs**, and has published its headline outside-spending series **without party committees** ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Fact_check/Is_hard_money_a_larger_share_of_political_spending_than_outside_money)). If it kept that definition this year, the $3.9 billion covers spending by super PACs, hybrid PACs, dark-money nonprofits and similar groups on election communications. It would not count money raised, cash on hand or transfers between committees. That is an inference from past practice, not something confirmed from the 2026 release.

## FEC data shows raised money far outruns ad spending

The FEC's own 18-month figures, January 1, 2025 to June 30, 2026, show how far apart these two measures can be ([FEC](https://www.fec.gov/updates/statistical-summary-of-18-month-campaign-activity-of-the-2025-2026-election-cycle/); [FEC table PAC1](https://www.fec.gov/resources/campaign-finance-statistics/2026/tables/pac/PAC1_2026_18m.pdf)).

| Committee type | Receipts | Disbursements | Cash on hand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Super PACs (independent expenditure-only) | $2,491.7M | $1,648.9M | $1,280.3M |
| Hybrid PACs (non-contribution accounts) | $4,229.7M | $3,501.7M | $1,021.4M |

Together these committees had raised about **$6.7 billion** by mid-2026 and held about **$2.3 billion in cash**. Yet **independent expenditures across all filers totalled only $643.4 million** over the same period, with super PACs accounting for $529.6 million and hybrid PACs for $97.3 million ([FEC](https://www.fec.gov/updates/statistical-summary-of-18-month-campaign-activity-of-the-2025-2026-election-cycle/)). The FEC totals are not directly comparable to Public Citizen's, because FEC gross receipts include transfers between committees and the hybrid PAC rows include contribution-limited accounts. The pattern still settles the question. Money raised and money spent on election ads are different flows. They also cover different periods: Public Citizen counts money raised through late October, while OpenSecrets projects spending for the full cycle. And they cover different sets of groups: super PACs and hybrid PACs in one case, every outside group in the other. A super PAC total of $4.2 billion or more raised next to a $3.9 billion outside-spending projection is consistent, not contradictory.

The site should not describe $4.2 billion as "70% of the $3.9 billion," should not call $4.2 billion "total super PAC money," and should not describe the Public Citizen figure as spending. The correct statement is:

> By late October, 515 donors who each gave at least $1 million had put $4.2 billion into super PACs. That is more than 70% of all the money super PACs had raised this cycle (Public Citizen). Separately, OpenSecrets projects that outside groups will spend $3.9 billion of the $11.9 billion total on the 2026 federal elections.

An optional follow-on sentence: "Money raised is not the same as money spent: super PACs also pass money to other groups and keep cash in reserve." Until someone opens the OpenSecrets release from a network that can reach it, the site should treat the $3.9 billion and 85% figures as reported by news outlets citing OpenSecrets.

## PPI's 13% is a share of $417.1 billion, not $445 billion

*The Whole Pie 2026* says mass incarceration comes "at a system-wide cost of **at least $445 billion each year**." Its footnote calls this "undoubtedly a low estimate" and sources it to PPI's ***Following the Money of Mass Incarceration 2026*** by Jacob Kang-Brown and Peter Wagner, published February 11, 2026 ([Whole Pie 2026](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/reports/pie2026.html)). That report describes the $445 billion as the cost to **"the government and families of system-involved people."** It builds the figure from two parts ([Following the Money 2026](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/reports/money2026.html)):

| Component | 2025 amount |
|---|---|
| Policing | $203.2B |
| Corrections | $115.8B |
| Immigration policing and detention | $54.3B |
| Judicial and legal (criminal share of courts) | $43.8B |
| **Government total** | **$417.1B** |
| Paid by individuals and families (fines and fees, bail premiums, asset forfeiture, phone calls, commissary) | $27.7B |
| **Sum** | **$444.8B, rounds to $445B** |

PPI does not spell out the addition in a single sentence, but the component figures and the "government and families" wording match it exactly. The $445 billion is therefore **not a government-only number**, and it covers **policing and courts, not only prisons and jails**. Courts are counted at half of total court spending, about $43.8 billion of $87.6 billion. Private prison revenue is treated as part of public spending rather than added on top. Lost income and any cost under $1 billion are left out. The figure also mixes data years. Federal amounts are FY2025 budget data plus annualised funding from P.L. 119-21, the 2025 reconciliation law. State and local amounts come from 2023 Census finance data and March 2024 payroll data. A few components are much older: health care uses 2015 data, and food and utilities use 2001 data ([Following the Money 2026](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/reports/money2026.html)). The accurate description is a current yearly estimate, not an actual 2025 total.

The immigration figures come from the same report. Whole Pie 2026 repeats them: immigration-related policing and detention "totaled **$54.3 billion in 2025 (up from $20 billion in 2017)**," and this makes up **13% of all government spending** on the criminal legal system ([Whole Pie 2026](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/reports/pie2026.html)). The arithmetic identifies the base of the 13%. **$54.3 billion ÷ $417.1 billion is 13.0%.** Measured against $445 billion, it would be 12.2%, which does not round to 13%. PPI calls the $54.3 billion "a lower bound." It is a budgeted and appropriated figure, compiled from ICE budget justifications, annualised reconciliation-law funding and a CBO estimate for National Guard deployments, not audited 2025 spending ([Following the Money 2026](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/reports/money2026.html)). Because the National Guard estimate is part of the figure, "government spending" is safer wording than "federal spending."

The "$20 billion in 2017" needs one more caution. It is in **2017 dollars**. Table 1 gives it as **$26.3 billion in 2025 dollars** ([Following the Money 2026](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/reports/money2026.html)). PPI's "2.7 times larger than in 2017" compares unadjusted dollars. After inflation, the increase is about 2.1 times. The site's wording copies PPI's, so it is not wrong, but it overstates real growth for a reader who assumes both numbers are in today's dollars. One more trap: Whole Pie 2026 contains a separate, unrelated 13%, the share of jail bookings tied to a missed court appearance. It should not be confused with the immigration share ([Whole Pie 2026](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/reports/pie2026.html)).

The correct statements, with *Following the Money of Mass Incarceration 2026* cited as the primary source for both, are:

> The Prison Policy Initiative estimates that mass incarceration costs governments and families at least $445 billion a year. That total counts policing, courts, prisons, jails and immigration detention, plus about $28 billion paid by people in the system and their families.

> Government spending on immigration policing and detention reached $54.3 billion in 2025, up from $20 billion in 2017 (about $26 billion in today's dollars). That is 13% of the $417 billion that governments spend on the criminal legal system.

If space is tight, the smallest correct change is to keep "13% of government spending" and add "($417 billion)" after it.

## Conclusion

Both pairs fail the same way, and neither failure is in the sources. Each number is accurate where it comes from. The error appears only when a page puts two numbers side by side without saying what each counts, and a reader assumes one is a share of the other. Public Citizen and OpenSecrets measure money flowing in different directions, over different windows, for different sets of groups. PPI's two figures sit on different totals inside the same table. In every case, naming the measure (raised versus spent, government-only versus government plus families) prevents the misreading, and that costs a few words. Two items remain open. The OpenSecrets $3.9 billion figure and its 85% growth rate need confirming against OpenSecrets' own release and methodology from a network that can reach the site. Public Citizen gives neither its data cutoff date nor the total its 73% is a share of. Neither gap changes the conclusion that the pairs do not contradict each other. The first affects how firmly the site can attribute the $3.9 billion figure.
