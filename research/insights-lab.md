# Insights and Lab: what the wave was, how power works, and practice

Checked: November 19 to 22, 2026. Verdict key: CONFIRMED (read on the source page or document) / CONFIRMED, SECONDARY (the figure is attributed to another body) / DISAGREE (the research file and the source differ; the source wins) / NOT USED (could not be confirmed, or not a fact we can stand behind).

Origin: five research files supplied by the site owner (a framework for civic power, a power-building framework, a rebuild of the Build Power architecture, "The Verified Empirical Anatomy of the 2026 Sea Change," and a "Rebuilding Civic Muscle" blueprint). Every figure below was re-read at the cited source. Where a file's figure differed from its source, the source is used and the difference is listed under DISAGREE. Quotes are kept under 15 words.

Method note: web-search summaries were used only to find pages. Several were wrong (one said Pew's four-point polling error was a hypothetical; the Pew page states it as a result). Every verdict below comes from opening the page.

## The scoreboard and the arena

- **House popular vote, D+13.** CONFIRMED. Brookings (Elaine Kamarck and William Galston, Nov. 6, 2026): "Democrats won the national popular vote by 13 points." CFR (Nov. 2026) calls it the second-largest midterm margin since 1942, behind 1974; 1958 was 11.9 and 1982 was 11.8. Wikipedia's running tally on Nov. 19 had 56.2% to 42.3% (13.9 points). The research file's "largest since 1974" is the same fact worded differently. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-democrats-won-big-and-why-2028-remains-an-open-question/ , https://www.cfr.org/articles/2026-election-by-the-numbers
- **Turnout 49.1% of the voting-eligible population.** CONFIRMED. CFR: second-highest midterm since 1914, behind 2018's 50 percent; highest Maine (65.7), lowest West Virginia (33.x). The estimate is preliminary.
- **Mid-decade redistricting backfired.** CONFIRMED. CFR: Republicans tilted 15 seats; California tilted 5 and a Utah ruling 1 toward Democrats. "Democrats picked up all six" and Republicans had won only two of the fifteen, with one uncalled and one headed to a December runoff (as of CFR's writing, about Nov. 10). Brookings adds: in Texas Republicans won one of five targeted seats (TX-32), and of the 19 seats the New York Times judged most affected, Democrats netted three. The research file's "nearly 20 more Democratic seats under neutral maps" is a New York Times estimate we could not open; NOT USED.
- **Turnout versus persuasion.** CONFIRMED. Brookings: compared with 2024, Abbott's vote fell 30% from Trump's total while Hinojosa exceeded Harris's by 8%; Donalds fell 32% and Jolly 14%; Ramaswamy fell 36% and Acton 5%; "only 6% of Republicans crossed over" in House races. Limit we add: the baseline is a presidential-year vote, which is always larger than a midterm vote, so the asymmetry is the finding, not the size of any single drop.
- **Polls understated Democrats.** CONFIRMED. Pew (Nov. 12): understated the Democratic margin by at least 2 points in 21 of 27 governor and Senate races; mean understatement about 4 points; 8 to 9 points in Texas, Georgia and the North Carolina Senate race; under 1 point in Kansas and the Minnesota governor race; Pennsylvania's governor race overstated Democrats by a little over 2. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/11/12/a-first-look-at-polling-performance-in-2026/
- **AP Voter Poll.** CONFIRMED, SECONDARY (AP story, syndicated). About 7 in 10 voters under 30 backed the Democrat in their House race, about 3 in 10 the Republican; about 1 in 10 voters were under 30; about half of young voters were "angry," against about a third of those 65 and older; most voters named the economy or the state of democracy as the top issue; corruption and the cost of living ranked "crucial" ahead of immigration enforcement, the Iran war and data centers. The research file's exact figures (72 to 25; 76% of ages 18 to 24; economy 39%) were not on the story page; NOT USED. https://www.news4jax.com/news/politics/2026/11/04/how-america-voted-in-the-2026-midterms-early-takeaways-from-the-ap-voter-poll/
- **Two surveys, two Hispanic numbers.** CONFIRMED, and a teaching point. Brookings: Republican House candidates got 29% of the Hispanic vote (48% for Trump in 2024). AP Voter Poll: about one-third, from about four in ten for Trump. Different surveys and samples give different numbers.

## Competing explanations

- **American Compass survey (Nov. 18, 2026).** DISAGREE with the research file. The survey is real (YouGov, 2,000 midterm voters; 1,931 voted for a major-party House candidate). Its final logit model, with a 95% bootstrap range: corruption D+6.9 (+3.4 to +12.2), Iran war D+3.2 (+1.5 to +5.4), tariffs D+0.5 (-0.3 to +1.6, not significant), immigration enforcement R+0.1 (-1.9 to +1.8, not significant), One Big Beautiful Bill D+0.1 (not significant). The authors say immigration "pushed voters in both directions," so its net effect was near zero. The research file said immigration enforcement was significant at +5.0 points; that is not the paper's final result and is NOT USED. The authors also warn that more than half of respondents gave the same answer on every issue, so many were committed partisans. American Compass is a conservative think tank; its authors argue against the claim that tariffs sank Republicans. https://americancompass.org/post-midterm-survey/
- **Third Way memo (Nov. 5, 2026).** CONFIRMED as an advocacy claim. Third Way, a centrist group, reports that red-state center-left Senate winners ran on average 21 points ahead of Harris's 2024 margin and 5 points ahead of their party's statewide House vote, while El-Sayed, Flanagan and Jackson ran 2, 3 and 10 points behind it; Pingree ran 11 ahead of Jackson, Benson 4 ahead of El-Sayed, Klobuchar 3 ahead of Flanagan. The comparison cannot separate candidate effects from state effects (Maine's incumbent, Michigan's primary). https://www.thirdway.org/memo/center-left-democrats-overperformed-and-delivered-the-majority
- **Bonica and Grumbach (The New Republic transcript, Nov. 6, 2026).** CONFIRMED. Two political scientists argue that massive Democratic turnout, particularly among Black voters and young voters, drove the results, and that national waves carry the day. The research file's "Boston Review interview" was not found; the New Republic transcript is the source used. https://newrepublic.com/article/217672/transcript-democratic-moderation-isnt-produced-wave
- **Georgia turnout (Brennan Center, Nov. 6, 2026).** CONFIRMED, with a limit. Preliminary voter file: the white-Black turnout gap was the lowest of any midterm in 20 years; 29% of white 2024 voters stayed home against 24% of Black 2024 voters; 93% of white 2026 voters had voted in 2024. The research file says advocates tied this to the Callais ruling; the Brennan analysis does not, so the causal link is NOT USED as fact. https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/surprising-turnout-rates-2026-midterms
- **Louisiana v. Callais.** CONFIRMED, SECONDARY. Decided April 2026, 6 to 3; Justice Alito's majority (with Roberts, Thomas, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh and Barrett) held Louisiana's second majority-Black district an unconstitutional racial gerrymander. https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-courts-callais-decision-sets-new-framework-for-racial-gerrymandering
- **Statewide ballot measures.** CONFIRMED. Ballotpedia: 164 certified in 40 states for 2026, 145 decided Nov. 3, the most in an even-numbered year since 2018, up 17% from 140 in 2022. DISAGREE: the research file says "53 citizen initiatives and 111 referrals"; 53 and 108 are the long-run averages for even-numbered years (161 together), not the 2026 split, so the split is NOT USED. Voter-identification measures were on the ballot in seven states; voters approved them in Nevada, North Carolina, Ohio and Oklahoma. Idaho voters defeated a citizen-initiated abortion-rights statute. https://ballotpedia.org/2026_ballot_measures
- **Local data-center ballot measures.** CONFIRMED. Ballotpedia: 57 measures, 53 decided; voters favored limiting data-center development in 52 (98%), by approving restrictions or opposing measures, or by rejecting measures that supported development; 47 of 57 were on Nov. 3; Ohio had 21. This replaces the site's earlier "40 of 41" and "44 of 45". https://ballotpedia.org/Data_center-related_ballot_measures,_2026
- **State legislatures.** CONFIRMED. Ballotpedia, Nov. 16, 11:30 a.m. ET: Democrats had won 46 and Republicans 39 of the 88 chambers on the ballot, 1 went to coalition or a tie, 2 were uncalled; 12 changed party control, all to Democrats. https://ballotpedia.org/State_legislative_elections,_2026
- **Trifectas.** CONFIRMED, and corrects the site. Ballotpedia (Nov. 19): today there are 23 Republican, 16 Democratic and 11 divided governments; as a result of the 2026 elections Democrats gain six trifectas and Republicans lose five, 40 trifectas in all; North Carolina was undetermined as of Nov. 18; the Democratic peak since 1992 was 18 (1993). The research file's "20 Democratic, 16 Republican, 9 divided" was a Nov. 12 snapshot. Our Build power note that said States Win's "16 to 23" trifecta claim was contradicted by Ballotpedia read the current table, not the post-election one; it is corrected. https://ballotpedia.org/State_government_trifectas
- **Corporate money.** CONFIRMED. Public Citizen (Oct. 29, 2026): about $737 million in corporate political spending, up 60% from the 2024 presidential cycle, 40% of the $1.8 billion corporations have spent on federal elections since Citizens United; crypto, online betting and Big Tech $391 million. DISAGREE: the research file's "AdImpact $12 billion" is not on AdImpact's pages; AdImpact projects $11.6 billion for 2025 to 2026 (above $11.2 billion in 2024 and $8.9 billion in 2022). The Money page already uses OpenSecrets' $11.9 billion. https://www.citizen.org/news/corporate-political-spending-hits-737-million-in-2026-midterms-60-higher-than-2024-presidential-election/ , https://adimpact.com/political-projections-26-updated
- **Elected utility commissions.** CONFIRMED. Ballotpedia News (Oct. 26, 2026): voters elect public service commissioners in 10 states; governors or legislatures appoint in 40; nine states elected commissioners Nov. 3; Republicans held majorities on all nine commissions up. Georgia's commission: Democrats won both seats, a 3 to 2 majority, the first Democratic control in 30 years (S&P Global and local reports). The research file's "over $1 billion shifted to residential customers" is a candidate's campaign claim; we found no independent check; NOT USED as fact (used as a Lab exercise). https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/10/26/nine-states-are-holding-public-service-commission-elections-in-2026-with-two-expected-to-be-battlegrounds/
- **2027 and 2028 calendar.** CONFIRMED. Ballotpedia: three governorships in 2027 (Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi; Republicans hold two, a Democrat one; Beshear and Reeves are term-limited); 11 in 2028. https://ballotpedia.org/Gubernatorial_elections,_2027
- **Governors.** CONFIRMED. Democrats 30 to Republicans 20, up from 24 (StockAnalysis, Nov. 16, 2026); all 36 races called, Alaska last on Nov. 13. The research file's "first gubernatorial majority in 16 years" matches that source; AP's "last gained a majority in 2006" is a different statement (when it was gained, not lost).

## Research behind the Lab

- **Hall and Deardorff, "Lobbying as Legislative Subsidy,"** American Political Science Review 100(1), Feb. 2006, pp. 69-84. CONFIRMED (bibliographic record and abstract): lobbying as a matching grant of policy information, political intelligence and labor to allied legislators, not vote-buying; groups lobby allies more than opponents.
- **Mansbridge, "Rethinking Representation,"** APSR 97(4), Nov. 2003, pp. 515-528. CONFIRMED: promissory, anticipatory, gyroscopic and surrogate representation.
- **Tsebelis, Veto Players: How Political Institutions Work,** Princeton University Press, 2002. CONFIRMED: more veto players, or more ideological distance between them, means more policy stability.
- **Han, How Organizations Develop Activists** (Oxford University Press, 2014) and **Han, McKenna and Oyakawa, Prisms of the People** (University of Chicago Press, 2021). CONFIRMED (publisher listings). **McAlevey, No Shortcuts** (Oxford University Press, 2016). CONFIRMED. Her date of death was not confirmed, so none is stated.
- **Kalla and Broockman, "The Minimal Persuasive Effects of Campaign Contact in General Elections,"** APSR 112(1), Feb. 2018, pp. 148-166. CONFIRMED: a meta-analysis of 40 earlier field experiments plus 9 new ones; best estimate of campaign contact's persuasive effect in general elections is zero, with rare exceptions.
- **Broockman and Kalla, Science, 2016 ("Durably reducing transphobia").** CONFIRMED: 56 canvassers, 255 voters in the treatment group and 246 in the control group in Miami-Dade County; effects lasted at least three months. DISAGREE: any "nine months" claim belongs to the retracted 2014 LaCour and Green paper.
- **Wineburg and McGrew, "Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise,"** Teachers College Record 121(11), 2019. CONFIRMED: 45 participants (10 historians, 10 fact checkers, 25 Stanford undergraduates); fact checkers left the page and opened new tabs; historians and students read vertically and were more often misled.

## NOT USED (could not be confirmed, or not a fact we can stand behind)

- "All nine Senate flips were in states Trump carried in 2024" (research file). Maine is the exception: it is one of the nine flips and Harris carried it. The site now says "eight of nine."
- "Immigration enforcement had an independent, significant effect (+5.0 points)." The American Compass final model shows no significant net effect (see above).
- "Relational contact rates of 86%," "a friend's text raises turnout 8.3 points," "15 million conversations by one platform," and "OUTVOTE 3.0 points." These come from vendor pages or papers we did not open; no peer-reviewed source was confirmed.
- "Deep canvassing effects equal to 14 years of attitude change." Not found in the study abstract we could confirm.
- "Nearly 20 more Democratic House seats under neutral maps." A New York Times estimate we could not open.
- "$12 billion AdImpact," "October alone $4.1 billion," "Musk nearly $300 million (Public Citizen)," "Osborn faced $37 million." Not found at the cited sources.
- Claims about the site owner's own tools (a software "enforcement engine," a social-media profile) in one research file. These describe the owner's projects, are not election facts, and are not published here.
- The young-men survey figures (more than 70% want equal caregiving, 30% already caregivers, and so on). A New America and NORC survey exists, but we could not match the research file's figures or the Guardian article it names.
