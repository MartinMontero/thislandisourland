# Build power: "Pick your lever" guide and winners scorecard

Checked: November 19, 2026. Verdict key: CONFIRMED / CONFIRMED, SECONDARY / NOT USED.

Origin: the same five research files behind the Insights and Lab page, using the parts that page did not (the Build Power playbooks for local issues, data and accountability, and the "substantive delivery scorecard"). Every statement below was re-read at the cited page. Web-search summaries were used only to find pages. ## Who decides: facts used on the page

- **Every state has a utility commission; ten elect it.** CONFIRMED. Ballotpedia News (Oct. 26, 2026): all 50 states have public service commissions that are the chief regulators of utilities including energy, telecommunications and water; voters elect commissioners in 10 states and governors or legislators appoint in 40. https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/10/26/nine-states-are-holding-public-service-commission-elections-in-2026-with-two-expected-to-be-battlegrounds/
- **Which ten.** CONFIRMED. Ballotpedia, "Public service commission elections, 2026": elections in 2026 were in Alabama, Arizona, Georgia, Louisiana, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota, Oklahoma and South Dakota; Mississippi also elects its commissioners but held no election this year. Democrats won a majority on one directly elected commission (Georgia). The Ballotpedia news item adds that South Carolina's commissioners are appointed by the Legislature. https://ballotpedia.org/Public_service_commission_elections,_2026
- **Names and websites of the ten commissions.** CONFIRMED (each site opened Nov. 19): Alabama Public Service Commission (psc.alabama.gov), Arizona Corporation Commission (azcc.gov), Georgia Public Service Commission (psc.ga.gov), Louisiana Public Service Commission (lpsc.louisiana.gov), Mississippi Public Service Commission (psc.ms.gov), Montana Public Service Commission (psc.mt.gov), Nebraska Public Service Commission (psc.nebraska.gov), North Dakota Public Service Commission (psc.nd.gov), Oklahoma Corporation Commission (oklahoma.gov/occ.html), South Dakota Public Utilities Commission (puc.sd.gov).
- **How a rate case works.** CONFIRMED, two state agencies. Minnesota PUC: a rate case starts when a regulated utility asks to raise rates; the commission generally has 10 months; the utility may collect interim rates subject to refund; an administrative law judge runs the process; "intervenors" can testify and question witnesses; the public can comment at hearings without intervening; the commission regulates investor-owned electric and gas utilities, not municipal utilities or cooperatives (a cooperative can opt in). Arkansas PSC: the utility must publish notice in newspapers; the commission must decide within 10 months of the application. The page says "in the two states we read," because timelines and steps differ by state. https://mn.gov/puc/activities/financial-analysis/general-rate-case/ , https://apsc.arkansas.gov/consumer-services/the-rate-case-process/
- **State override of local land-use rules.** CONFIRMED, one scholar's legal analysis. Kenneth Stahl, The Urban Lawyer 50(2), 2020 (American Bar Association): local governments have historically made most land-use decisions; he concludes that home rule does not protect land use from state preemption and that appellate courts have held states may preempt local land-use decisions. This is an argument about the law, labeled as such. https://www.americanbar.org/groups/state_local_government/resources/urban-lawyer/50-2/home-rule-state-preemption-local-land-use-control/
- **Governors' tools.** CONFIRMED. National Governors Association: governors use executive orders, executive budgets, legislative proposals and vetoes; many can line-item veto appropriations; legislatures can usually override vetoes by a supermajority; many appointments require legislative confirmation; a majority of governors appoint state judges; the scope of power varies by state. https://www.nga.org/governors/powers-and-authority/
- **Senate: confirmations.** CONFIRMED. Senate.gov: the president nominates, "by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate" appoints, judges and other officers (Article II, section 2). https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/nominations.htm
- **Congress: investigations.** CONFIRMED. Senate.gov: the authority of Congress to investigate is an implied constitutional power that the Supreme Court has established as essential to its legislative responsibilities. https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/investigations.htm
- **Money and impeachment.** CONFIRMED. Constitution text at the National Archives: all revenue bills originate in the House (Art. I, §7); no money is drawn from the Treasury except by appropriations made by law (Art. I, §9); the House has the sole power of impeachment (Art. I, §2). https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript
- **Open meetings.** CONFIRMED, limited. The Reporters Committee's Open Government Guide describes itself as a compendium of every state's open records and open meetings laws. We did not find one source saying every state requires the same things, so the page says to check your state's section. https://www.rcfp.org/open-government-guide/
- **Finding officials.** CONFIRMED. USAGov: pages for state governments and governors and for finding federal, state and local elected officials. https://www.usa.gov/state-local-governments , https://www.usa.gov/elected-officials

## The scorecard

- The 29 winners and their listed promises come from the site's own profiles (each with its sources and date). Those promises are what campaign coverage reported, not a guarantee of what each will do. The page does not grade anyone; the reader records what they find.
- Of the 29 profiles, 28 are Democrats and one (the Los Angeles mayor) holds a nonpartisan office. To keep the tool useful for anyone, it lets the reader add any official and any promise.
