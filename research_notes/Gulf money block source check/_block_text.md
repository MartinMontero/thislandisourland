# Text of the gulf-money block (site copy to be verified)


    Analysis · Foreign government money and the presidency
    Gulf money and Trump’s businesses: what the records show, and what they do not
    This is an analysis block, not a news item. It asks one question: did money from foreign governments to businesses tied to the president produce policy decisions? The payments are documented. We found no court finding, inspector general report, indictment, plea or jury verdict that says any decision was made because of a payment. Each claim below is labelled by who made it.
        Documented
          - A January 4, 2024 report by House Oversight Committee Democratic staff, “White House for Sale,” counts at least $7.8 million from at least 20 foreign governments to four Trump properties over roughly two years of the presidency. It is the minority’s report, not a finding of the committee. About 71% ($5,572,548) is China, mostly an estimate of rent paid by a Chinese bank for Trump Tower space. Saudi Arabia, Qatar and the UAE together are $1,146,391, about 15%. The Saudi figure ($615,422) is itself an estimate.
          - Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund committed $2 billion to Jared Kushner’s Affinity Partners, with a 1.25% fee on committed funds (Senate Finance Committee Democrats). The New York Times reported in April 2022, from the fund’s own minutes, that its screening panel objected, citing the inexperience of Affinity’s management, and that the board, led by the Crown Prince, overruled it days later.
          - The administration notified Congress on May 24, 2019 of emergency arms sales worth more than $8 billion to Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Jordan. Trump vetoed the Yemen war-powers resolution on April 16, 2019 and three resolutions disapproving those sales on July 24, 2019. The Senate did not override either. The State Department’s inspector general later found the sales were carried out in accordance with the Arms Export Control Act, and faulted how civilian-casualty risk was assessed.
          - Tom Barrack was indicted in July 2021 on charges of acting as an unregistered agent of the UAE and was acquitted on every count on November 4, 2022. George Nader pleaded guilty to conspiracy to defraud the United States over concealed foreign contributions; Elliott Broidy pleaded guilty to conspiracy to violate the Foreign Agents Registration Act over lobbying for Jho Low and was pardoned in January 2021.
          - The United States and Saudi Arabia signed a civil nuclear (Section 123) agreement on July 22, 2026. It was sent to Congress on September 14 for a 90-day review, and disapproval resolutions were introduced in the House on September 17 and the Senate on October 19.
        Alleged or attributed, not established
          - Affinity’s fees. Senate Democrats estimate “as much as $157 million” from foreign clients and $87 million from the Saudi government through about mid-2024. A March 2026 letter from Sen. Ron Wyden and Rep. Robert Garcia adds an estimated $39 million from the Saudi government in 2025 and “more than $110 million” since 2021. These are minority-party estimates. The letters say Affinity may be acting as an unregistered foreign agent; we found no Justice Department or court finding of a violation, and no Affinity response.
          - Inspector general Steve Linick’s firing. Democrats tie it to his review of the arms sales. The State Department says it was over management and ethics.
          - Kushner and the arms figure. ABC News, citing anonymous sources, reported that Kushner pushed officials to inflate the “$110 billion” Saudi deal. The New York Times, also from anonymous sources, reported that he advised the Crown Prince after the Khashoggi murder.
          - The 2017 nuclear plan. House Democratic staff reports, citing whistleblowers, alleged that a group of retired generals and executives (IP3) pushed a plan to build Saudi reactors that skipped normal review. House Republican staff answered in July 2019 that the record “does not show impropriety.” Barrack was never made envoy and no reactor or nuclear material was transferred.
      What the dates and documents do not support
        - The blockade of Qatar began on June 5, 2017. The Oval Office contacts by Broidy and Nader that sources document are from October to December 2017, so they cannot have started it. At Barrack’s trial, Treasury Secretary Mnuchin testified that Barrack’s position was clearly in support of Qatar.
        - Whether Broidy and Nader caused Secretary of State Tillerson’s firing is contested. Nader urged it, and Trump gave other reasons.
        - In March 2018, most of the documented Saudi hotel spending came before the Crown Prince’s March 20 visit. The $1.3 billion artillery sale was notified on April 5, and about $1 billion in anti-tank missiles on March 22 and 23.
        - The Democratic hotel report itself does not say the UAE stays caused any policy outcome, and for China says only that the payments created conflicts of interest.
      So the evidence sits at documented money and requests on one side and documented decisions on the other, with timing in between. No source read ties a specific payment to a specific decision. Democrats allege influence; Republicans and Kushner’s lawyers deny it.
      Live items, each an allegation or a pending process
        - The Saudi nuclear agreement’s 90-day congressional review ends about December 13, 2026.
        - The watchdog group CREW sued on October 14, 2026 to have Kushner, an unpaid envoy who was not confirmed by the Senate, treated as a government employee. The White House calls the suit unserious, and his attorney says it is without merit.
        - The March 2026 letters say Affinity was seeking at least $5 billion more in foreign capital and asked for a response by April 2. No response was found.
    How this was checked: a claim-by-claim review of an outside research file against sources, with a second pass on November 13, 2026. Several documents were read through summaries or archive copies. Not read: the New York Times originals, the Barrack verdict form, Affinity’s filings, and the Senate Finance page of September 2024, so the $157 million and $87 million figures rest on the March 2026 letter’s footnote.
